What 250 hand-labeled bugs taught me about triage

+

Oct 2020 · 5 min read

Building a bug classifier with Mozilla's dev team, and why the boring part — the labeling — mattered more than the model.

Mozilla's issue tracker gets more bug reports than any triage team can read carefully. Most reports are duplicates, misfiled, or missing the one detail that tells you which component actually owns the problem. The thesis brief was simple to state and hard to do: build something that could route incoming reports to the right area automatically, well enough that a human triager would trust it.

I started where most people don't want to: labeling. I went through 250+ historical bug reports by hand, assigning each one a category based on the same signals a human triager would use — title, description, stack traces where present, affected component. It was slow and unglamorous, and it's also the reason the rest of the project worked. A classifier is only as good as the ground truth you give it, and there wasn't an existing labeled dataset that matched how Mozilla's team actually triaged.

With that dataset, I treated it as a text classification problem: turn free-text bug reports into features a model can use, then compare a few standard approaches (SVM, CNN, decision trees) rather than assume the fanciest one wins. The naive baseline — routing everything to the most common component — sat around 70% accuracy, which sounds better than it is; it just reflects how skewed the categories are. The trained classifier pushed that to 92% accuracy, with 92% precision and 89% recall on held-out data, and cut the manual classification time for new reports by roughly 80%.

The gap between 70% and 92% wasn't a smarter algorithm. It was feature engineering — figuring out which parts of a bug report actually carry signal — and being honest about class imbalance instead of optimizing for a metric that rewarded ignoring the rare, important categories. The model that shipped was less exciting than the one I wanted to build first. It was also the one that worked.

What I'd tell someone starting a similar project: budget real time for labeling, distrust your first accuracy number until you've checked it against a sane baseline, and pick evaluation metrics that reflect what the humans downstream actually care about — for triage, missing a rare-but-critical category is worse than a wrong guess on a common one.

Idempotency, not retries: fixing a migration that kept corrupting itself

+

Mar 2024 · 6 min read

500 GB, a source connection that dropped at random, and why "just retry it" was making things worse, not better.

At Cluster Reply I owned a migration: roughly 500 GB of data moving from a legacy provider into Azure, feeding an IoT diagnostics platform that field engineers relied on. The problem wasn't volume, it was reliability. The source connection dropped at random — sometimes mid-batch, sometimes mid-record — and the first version of the pipeline handled that the obvious way: catch the error, retry.

That's what broke it. A retry after a partial failure doesn't know what already landed on the other side. Some batches got written twice, producing duplicate records. Others got abandoned halfway, leaving the destination with incomplete data and no clean way to tell "finished" from "half-finished" just by looking at it. Retrying made the pipeline more resilient to the symptom and worse at the actual job: getting a correct copy of the data across.

The fix was to make the whole transfer safe to redo from any point, instead of just catching errors and trying again. Two changes did most of the work. First, deduplication keys: every record carried an identifier that made a second write of the same record a no-op, so a retry could never double anything up, no matter how far the first attempt had gotten. Second, checkpointed, resumable batches built on Azure Durable Functions — the transfer tracked its own progress, so a dropped connection meant the next attempt picked up from the last completed checkpoint instead of restarting the whole batch, or worse, the whole migration.

After that, a dropped connection stopped being an incident. It was just a batch that resumed a few seconds later, with the same result whether it failed once or ten times: the destination ends up correct, not "probably correct if nothing went wrong."

The lesson holds outside this one migration too: if a network call can fail partway through and "just retry" is your whole plan for that, you don't have a fix — you have a bug that hasn't shown up at scale yet. Idempotency has to be built in at the record level. You can't bolt it on with a try/catch afterward.
